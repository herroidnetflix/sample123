DMCA takedowns and strikes

The DMCA is a US law that platforms such as YouTube, Instagram and Facebook use to handle copyright complaints. It can affect creators anywhere.

How a takedown works

  1. The owner sends the platform a notice about your content.
  2. The platform removes or disables it, and may record a warning or strike.
  3. If you believe the removal is wrong, you can send a counter-notice.
  4. The platform may restore the content after a waiting period unless the owner starts a court case.

What a valid notice contains

  • Identification of the copyrighted work
  • The location of the content (URL)
  • Contact details of the complainant
  • A good-faith statement that the use is not authorised
  • A statement, under penalty of perjury, that the notice is accurate and the sender is authorised
  • A signature

Counter-notice

Send one only if the content is yours, you have permission, or you honestly believe it was removed by mistake. It is a legal statement and includes consent to a US court's jurisdiction, so a false one can create serious trouble.

Claim vs strike

On YouTube, a Content ID claim lets the owner monetise, track or block a video, and it does not count as a strike. A copyright strike is a formal penalty. Three active strikes can lead to channel termination. Rules change, so read YouTube's current copyright policy.

If you receive a notice

  1. Read who complained and which content it covers.
  2. Remove the content if you lack permission, and do not re-upload it.
  3. Dispute only if you are confident, and keep your proof.
  4. Contact the owner if you want a licence.
  5. Seek legal advice if the matter is serious.

Knowingly sending a false takedown or counter-notice can have legal consequences, so be accurate when reporting too.
